Parallel BPE throughput

A byte-level BPE encoder built on snicholls-core's merge and ts-moveables' work-claiming parallel_for, token for token what tiktoken gives, raced against eleven other open-source encoders. On GPT-4's vocabulary it is level with the fastest, toktok, at 32 threads and on new text, and second to it from 2 to 16 threads; with a vocabulary of your own it is first on every measure; and it is the only one that spreads a single text across every core.

Correctness

  • 778 of 778 documents give exactly tiktoken's tokens, for both vocabularies, serially and in parallel, with chunks from 4 KB up to the default size, with the memo cold and warm. The benchmark refuses to time anything until every check passes.
  • The whole corpus as one text, cut across the pool, gives exactly its serial tokens. Every document decodes back to its bytes.
  • The splitter's ASCII fast path agrees with the character-by-character code it hands over to at every position of the corpus and of 300,000 adversarial texts (47 million positions, both patterns); two bugs planted on purpose are both caught.
  • Every other encoder shown also gives tiktoken's tokens on every document, on every path timed, so all the rows are doing the same work.

Where the speed comes from

  • The split pattern as code, ASCII 16 bytes at a time. tiktoken spends most of its time in fancy-regex. Here the patterns are written out; on ASCII each decision depends on one byte's kind, read once from a 256-entry table, and letter, space and punctuation runs are measured with SSE2. Anything non-ASCII goes to the original code, which stays as the reference. About 420 MB/s on one thread.
  • A memo of short pieces. 99% of pieces are 15 bytes or fewer, 63,000 distinct in this corpus. Each thread keeps a 4 MB table of them with their tokens, keyed without reading past the piece (exact, and clean under AddressSanitizer); one- and two-byte pieces come from tables built with the encoder. The idea is toktok's.
  • Inlined. Forcing the splitter and the per-piece code into the encode loop bought more than any table: as calls, their live values went through memory.
  • Cutting one text safely. A long text is cut where the whole-text split is proved to start a piece, and workers claim the chunks from a shared cursor on the work-stealing pool, so one text uses every core and still gives its serial tokens.
  • The merge. For the 7% of pieces that are not one token: snicholls-core's bpe_encode_piece, tiktoken's byte_pair_merge step for step, allocation-free and constexpr, behind a 4 MB per-thread cache. Pieces of 1 KB or more take core's O(n log n) heap merge, about 100 times faster at 128 KB.

Limits of these numbers

  • One corpus, mostly source code. Prose, other languages and other scripts will shift every row; memos help most where pieces repeat, which is why the cold column is there.
  • One machine, shared with other work while it ran; each cell is the best of five full passes for that reason. Linux and ARM are still to be measured.
  • Ours was built with -march=native, as the Rust crates were with -C target-cpu=native. The Python rows include building Python lists; the native rows are the fair comparison for the encoders themselves.
  • The Unicode classes come from the regex module's tables and are checked through tiktoken's tokens on this corpus. Code points assigned in a newer Unicode version than tiktoken's could split differently.
  • Only the cl100k and GPT-4 split patterns are supported; o200k's is not yet, and neither are special tokens.
